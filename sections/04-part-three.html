Part Three

Writing Effective Prompts

The prompt is the single most influential control in SFT Studio Pro. A well-built prompt does more for your output than any slider, and learning to write one is a craft this part will teach you quickly.

3.1  The Anatomy of a Prompt

Strong prompts are built from four ingredients, in a reliable order:

  1. Subject — who or what the image is about: a lighthouse keeper
  2. Setting — where and when: on a basalt cliff at blue hour
  3. Light — the quality and direction of illumination: lantern glow against the last of the dusk
  4. Style — the rendering treatment: painterly realism, muted maritime palette

Joined together: a lighthouse keeper on a basalt cliff at blue hour, lantern glow against the last of the dusk, painterly realism, muted maritime palette. That single sentence will outperform a paragraph of scattered adjectives.

3.2  Camera and Composition Language

When framing matters, say so explicitly. The studio understands conventional photography and cinematography vocabulary:

  • Shot size: extreme wide shot, wide shot, medium shot, close-up, macro
  • Angle: eye level, low angle, high angle, bird's-eye view, Dutch angle
  • Lens character: 35mm cinematic, 85mm portrait, shallow depth of field, deep focus
  • Composition: centred composition, rule of thirds, leading lines, negative space

Tip. One or two camera terms per prompt is plenty. Stacking every lens term you know produces muddled frames, not better ones.

3.3  Negative Prompts

The negative prompt tells the studio what to keep out of the frame. Use it sparingly and specifically: watermarks, text, extra fingers, motion blur. Long, angry lists of exclusions restrict the model far more than they guide it — three to six well-chosen terms is the professional range.

3.4  Prompt Weight and Order

Terms placed earlier in a prompt carry slightly more influence than terms placed later. Lead with what the image must not get wrong — usually the subject — and let refinements trail behind. When two ideas compete, the one written first usually wins.

3.5  Building a Prompt Library

SFT Studio Pro lets you star any prompt from the inspector and save it to your prompt library with Ctrl+D. Treat the library as a working notebook: save the prompts that produced results you loved, name them honestly, and you will accumulate a personal vocabulary that gets better with every session.

3.6  Common Prompt Mistakes

  • Contradictions — empty street, crowded market forces the model to guess which you meant.
  • Describing the unwanted — no rain puts rain in the model's mind; use the negative prompt instead.
  • Style soup — naming five artists and four mediums in one prompt averages them into mush. Pick one direction and commit.
  • Under-specifying light — light shapes mood more than any other single term. When a prompt is flat, rewrite the light first.
